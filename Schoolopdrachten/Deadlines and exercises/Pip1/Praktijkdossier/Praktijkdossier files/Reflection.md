From relatively early on in my placement, I was largely left to my own devices when it came to preparing and delivering lessons. Having started with almost no practical teaching experience, I had expected slightly more guidance and support in learning how to navigate my new responsibilities. In practice, much of the guidance I received consisted of brief emails outlining what needed to be included in my lessons or which administrative tasks had to be completed, often arriving less than 48 hours before the relevant lesson. These instructions generally provided little explanation of how I was expected to approach these tasks or incorporate them into my teaching. I found this quite draining at times, as I was simultaneously trying to understand the curriculum, develop my teaching skills and familiarise myself with the school's procedures.

As a result, I became increasingly reliant on my own initiative and the knowledge of more experienced colleagues. I actively sought information, asked questions and observed how other teachers approached lesson planning, classroom management and their responsibilities within the school. This also explains why I initially placed such a strong emphasis on structure and preparation. Having a detailed lesson plan gave me something to fall back on when I was uncertain about how to approach a situation.

Although I would have appreciated more structured guidance, this experience ultimately helped me develop a strong sense of ownership over my lessons and professional responsibilities. I learned to take initiative, find solutions independently and take responsibility for the quality of my teaching. Looking back, I believe this independence has been an important factor in my development, even if the circumstances that encouraged it were not always ideal.

My first fully independent grammar lesson with 2 VWO on 17 September demonstrated that lesson preparation and classroom management are two very different challenges. I deliberately chose grammar because I expected its clear structure and learning objectives to make the lesson easier to manage. While I was reasonably satisfied with my instruction, I struggled with maintaining classroom cohesion, particularly during independent work. I realised that knowing the material and explaining it effectively did not automatically mean I could manage a classroom. I did expect this to be a hurdle because in the partial lessons I already gave I already noticed some struggles with classroom management
Over the following weeks, I became more confident in designing and delivering lessons. My Chapter 1 test recap lesson with 2 VWO on 28 October was an important moment in this development. I used a diagnostic LessonUp quiz to identify students who needed additional instruction and those who were ready for a more challenging assignment. This allowed me to differentiate my teaching, and students responded positively to having activities suited to their needs. I was particularly satisfied with this lesson because I felt more confident organising different learning activities simultaneously.
I also started experimenting with more active teaching methods. During my 30 Seconds vocabulary lesson with 3 HAVO, students practised describing and recognising vocabulary in English. However, I noticed that several students struggled with speaking English and interpreting the instructions. This reinforced an observation I had made during the Chapter 1 test, where students frequently appeared to misunderstand questions because they had not read them carefully.
To address this, I introduced the TASK strategy in both 2 VWO and 3 HAVO. TASK stands for Task word, Answer form, Special conditions and Keep checking. Instead of simply reminding students to read carefully, I wanted to provide them with a concrete method for approaching written instructions. This represents an important development in my teaching, as I am increasingly using observations of students' difficulties to inform my lesson planning.

Regarding the pedagogical competencies, I consider building positive relationships with students one of my strengths. I enjoy interacting with students and showing an interest in their experiences. My werkplekbegeleider has also recognised my enthusiasm, preparation, visual support and willingness to learn.
Looking back, I believe most of my professional development has come from practical experience, experimentation and reflecting on situations that did not go as expected.
One of my most significant realisations concerns my tendency to avoid confrontation. I naturally prefer maintaining positive relationships with students, but I have discovered that this sometimes prevents me from addressing disruptive behaviour effectively. Particularly in 3 HAVO, I occasionally allowed situations to continue longer than I should have because I was reluctant to intervene.
On 5 November 2026, I consciously changed my approach by becoming more direct and assertive when correcting unwanted behaviour. Students appeared to respond positively, although I recognise that one lesson is insufficient to determine whether this improvement will last. Nevertheless, it helped me understand that establishing authority does not necessarily conflict with maintaining positive relationships.
I believe it is important to separate students from their behaviour. When I give disciplinary measures, I prefer to speak with the students afterwards and explain that I disapprove of their actions rather than them personally. I want students to understand that a correction does not change how I see them as individuals. For me, being approachable and maintaining clear boundaries are both essential aspects of effective teaching.

I have also become more aware of differences between classes. I generally prefer teaching 2 VWO because I experience greater engagement and a stronger work ethic compared to 3 HAVO. However, I recognise that this preference presents a professional challenge. I need to develop strategies to engage students who are less naturally motivated rather than allowing their attitude to influence my own expectations.
My experiences have also helped me connect educational theory to practice. For example, my differentiated test recap lesson demonstrated the value of formative assessment. By checking students' existing knowledge before instruction, I could adapt activities to their individual needs. Similarly, the TASK strategy uses explicit instruction and scaffolding to support students in developing a skill they can eventually apply independently.
The 30 Seconds activity also taught me that active learning does not automatically result in successful learning. Students need sufficient guidance and clear instructions to participate effectively. This has encouraged me to consider more carefully whether my chosen activities genuinely support the intended learning objectives.

Based on my experiences and self-assessment, I have identified two main learning objectives for the next stage of my development.

### Learning Objective 1: Developing Consistent Classroom Management

I want to become more confident and consistent in addressing disruptive behaviour, particularly in 3 HAVO. My goal is to intervene earlier, communicate expectations clearly and follow through on corrections without unnecessary hesitation.
To achieve this, I intend to continue applying the more assertive approach I introduced on 5 November. Over the coming weeks, I will reflect on how I respond to disruptions and whether my interventions are effective. I also want to ask my supervisor to observe a lesson specifically focusing on my classroom management and provide concrete feedback. From experience this might take some direct instruction towards my supervisor. 
This objective is important because I want to become a teacher who maintains positive relationships with students while also providing a structured and productive learning environment. I believe that being approachable and being authoritative should complement rather than contradict each other.

### Learning Objective 2: Improving Instruction and Evaluation of Learning

My second goal is to become more systematic in checking whether students understand instructions and achieve the intended learning objectives.
I want to continue developing the TASK strategy and incorporate regular checks for understanding into my lessons. Rather than assuming students understand an assignment, I will ask them to explain what is expected before beginning. I also intend to make greater use of diagnostic questions and exit tickets to identify learning difficulties. I'll simultaneously ask other teachers about their experiences with students having difficulty understanding Exercise task descriptions and test questions. I'll take their responses into consideration when finetuning this overarching teaching goal 
Over the coming lessons, I will evaluate whether students can apply the TASK strategy independently and use their responses to determine whether further instruction is necessary. This should help me make more informed decisions about lesson planning and differentiation.
I want to become a teacher who not only explains subject matter clearly but also recognises when students struggle and adapts instruction accordingly. 

### Conclusion

I want my students to understand that English, like any school subject, is only one part of a much greater whole. Although I have a genuine love for the English language and its literature, I recognise that my students have their own interests, ambitions and perspectives. Not everyone will share my enthusiasm for the subject, nor should I expect them to.
For me, English is not an end in itself, but also a means of developing skills that extend far beyond the classroom. Through language learning, I want students to develop their ability to communicate, think critically, express themselves and understand perspectives different from their own. These are skills that can benefit them regardless of the paths they eventually choose.
I also believe that teaching involves recognising the individual behind the student. Every classroom consists of different personalities, each with their own experiences, challenges and aspirations. I want to contribute to their personal development by showing genuine interest, offering advice when appropriate and approaching them with goodwill and respect.
Ultimately, I do not expect every student to leave my classroom with a passion for English literature. However, I hope they leave with something valuable, whether that is greater confidence, a new perspective, improved communication skills or simply the feeling that someone took an interest in their development. **I want to teach English, but more importantly, I want to contribute positively to the people my students are becoming.**
