When I began my teaching placement in September 2026, I had completed a Bachelor's degree in English Language but had almost no experience planning and teaching lessons independently. I was given responsibility for my classes relatively early and had expected more guidance. Instead, much of the information I received came through brief emails, sometimes less than 48 hours before a lesson, listing content or administrative tasks without much explanation of how to approach them. Trying to understand the curriculum, school procedures and classroom expectations simultaneously was draining.
At the same time, these circumstances pushed me to take initiative. I asked experienced colleagues questions, observed how they handled lessons and tried to understand the school's routines and expectations. Detailed lesson plans and visual overviews gave me something reliable to return to when I was unsure how to respond in class. The experience developed my resourcefulness and sense of ownership.

My first fully independent Past Simple lesson with 2 VWO on 17 September exposed the difference between knowing how to explain content and knowing how to manage a class. I chose grammar because its objectives seemed straightforward. I was reasonably satisfied with my instruction, but the independent working phase was difficult: a few disruptions were enough to affect the atmosphere, and I was unsure when and how firmly to intervene. Earlier partial lessons had hinted at this problem.
By 28 October, I felt more capable of adapting a lesson to the students in front of me. During a Chapter 1 test recap with 2 VWO, I used a diagnostic LessonUp quiz to identify who needed further explanation and who could begin a creative assignment. Students responded positively to having different routes, and I was pleased that I could provide targeted instruction without requiring everyone to repeat material they already understood. This marked a change in my approach.
I also began experimenting with more active learning. In a 30 Seconds vocabulary lesson with 3 HAVO, students had to describe and guess words in English. Many struggled to express themselves, and some had difficulty following the written rules. I recognised the second issue from the Chapter 1 test, where students appeared to rush into answering questions without reading them carefully. In response, I introduced TASK in both 2 VWO and 3 HAVO: Task word, Answer form, Special conditions and Keep checking. Instead of merely telling students to read more carefully, I wanted to give them a usable method.


Most of my learning has taken place through practice, experimentation and reflection when things did not go as expected. One recurring pattern has been my reluctance to confront students. I want to maintain good relationships, but I have sometimes confused avoiding conflict with maintaining a positive atmosphere. In 3 HAVO especially, this could mean letting distractions continue longer than I should. On 5 November, I consciously addressed unwanted behaviour more directly. Students appeared to respond, although I know that one lesson does not establish a lasting change.
I also believe that correcting behaviour need not damage a relationship. After a disciplinary measure, I prefer to speak with the student and explain why the behaviour was unacceptable. I want students to know that the correction concerns their actions, not their worth as people. This helps me reconcile the teacher I naturally am, approachable and interested in individuals, with the teacher I need to become: someone who establishes clear boundaries.
The contrast between 2 VWO and 3 HAVO has made me examine my own expectations. I generally enjoy teaching 2 VWO more because I experience greater engagement and willingness to work. Yet different groups should not receive different levels of commitment from me. If a class is less motivated, I need to find more effective ways of engaging them instead of interpreting that difference as a fixed characteristic.
My lesson choices have also helped me connect theory and practice. The diagnostic quiz used formative assessment to guide differentiated instruction. TASK uses explicit instruction and scaffolding to make an often-overlooked skill visible before expecting independence. The 30 Seconds lesson reminded me that participation alone is not proof of learning: an engaging activity still requires clear modelling and checks for understanding. I now ask not only whether students enjoyed an activity, but what they learned.
I sometimes found the feedback available to me limited in detail, but I have learned to seek colleagues' perspectives and analyse my own decisions. I need to test whether changes work.

##### Learning Goal 1: Consistent Classroom Management
My first goal is to develop consistent classroom management, especially in 3 HAVO. In upcoming lessons, I will state expectations clearly, intervene earlier and follow through calmly. After each lesson, I will record a specific moment when I corrected a disruption effectively or hesitated, along with what happened afterwards. I will request an observation focused on this goal and compare that feedback with my own notes over several lessons.
##### Learning Goal 2: Clearer Instructions and Evaluation
My second goal is to improve how I teach instructions and evaluate understanding. I will continue modelling TASK, ask students to explain requirements before starting assignments and use short diagnostic questions or exit tickets. I will look for evidence that students can apply the strategy without prompts, and I will discuss recurring difficulties with colleagues. Their observations should help me refine the approach rather than assuming that introducing a strategy means it has been learned.

I love the English language and its literature, but I do not see English as an end in itself. Every student brings different interests and ambitions to the classroom. I want English to be a means of developing confidence, communication, critical thinking and openness to other perspectives, skills that matter beyond my subject.
I hope students remember not only what they learned, but also that I took an interest in them. Ultimately, I want to teach English while contributing positively to the people my students are becoming.
